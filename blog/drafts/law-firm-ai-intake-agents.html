Two new-client calls came in on a Tuesday afternoon while every attorney was in depositions. Both went to voicemail. By the time someone returned the calls — 90 minutes later — both prospects had already signed with competing firms they'd found on Google.

That's not a hypothetical. It's a real scenario that plays out at law firms every week, and it illustrates the core problem with traditional client intake: the bottleneck isn't finding leads — it's processing them fast enough to keep them. This is the problem AI agents are built to solve, and the law firms deploying them in 2026 are seeing results that are hard to argue with: 30–50% lifts in qualified-lead capture, 8–15 hours of senior attorney time recovered per week, and intake costs that are a fraction of what a part-time paralegal runs.

Here's a detailed look at how one mid-sized personal injury firm restructured their entire client intake operation using AI agents — what they built, how it works, what it actually changed, and where they still rely on humans.

The Intake Bottleneck Every Law Firm Has

A typical mid-sized personal injury firm gets 300–500 inbound calls per month. The breakdown looks something like this:

  • ~30% are clearly out of scope — wrong jurisdiction, wrong practice area, statute-barred
  • ~25% need a conflict check before an attorney can even talk to the caller
  • ~20% have basic questions the firm's website or a paralegal could answer
  • ~25% are qualified leads — and they're hitting voicemail at 8 PM on a Friday

The math is brutal. Senior attorneys end up spending hours per week on intake calls that should never have reached them, while the genuinely promising cases — the ones with real case value and urgency — sit in a voicemail queue until Monday morning. By then, those callers have already made a decision.

The firm in this case study had a specific version of this problem. They ran a 6-attorney PI shop with one full-time intake coordinator. Their intake coordinator was excellent — but she worked business hours, took vacations, and couldn't handle volume spikes. After-hours calls went to voicemail 100% of the time. Their conversion rate on after-hours leads was under 20%, while business-hours leads converted at nearly 60%. The gap was costing them cases they didn't even know they were losing.

What an AI Intake Agent Actually Does

An AI intake agent in 2026 is not a phone tree, a chatbot widget on a website, or a voicemail transcription service. It's a voice-first system that holds a real conversation, gathers structured information, makes routing decisions, and takes action in your case management system — all in the time it used to take a human to say "Can you hold for one moment?"

For a law firm, a well-built intake agent handles six specific jobs:

1. Answers within seconds, around the clock

The agent picks up the firm's main line in under 2 seconds, every time. No hold music, no voicemail, no "we'll call you back." This alone is the single biggest driver of lead conversion improvement — the firm that answers first wins a disproportionate share of cases.

2. Runs structured intake

The agent gathers everything a human paralegal would: caller name and contact info, jurisdiction, nature of the case, date of incident, opposing party, insurance carrier, prior representation, and any statute-of-limitations flags. It follows the firm's exact intake script and asks follow-up questions if answers are vague — "You mentioned you were injured in December — do you know the exact date?"

3. Performs real-time conflict checks

This is the feature that separates legal-grade AI agents from generic AI receptionists. The agent searches the caller's name and opposing party against the firm's case management system (Clio, MyCase, Filevine, PracticePanther, and others all have integrations) before scheduling anything. If a potential conflict surfaces, the agent halts scheduling and escalates to a paralegal. It also logs the conflict-check trail for ethical compliance. This isn't optional — skipping it creates malpractice exposure.

4. Routes by case type

Different cases need different attorneys. The agent handles multi-practice routing — PI calls go to the PI partner's calendar, criminal cases get flagged as urgent and routed to the criminal attorney, family law matters go to the appropriate associate. Each route has its own intake script and priority rules. A caller facing an imminent court date gets treated differently than someone who just started shopping for an estate planning attorney.

5. Books consultations directly

Once a caller is qualified and conflicts are cleared, the agent books the consultation into the attorney's calendar in real time. No callback needed, no coordination overhead, no double-booking. The caller leaves the call with a confirmed appointment.

6. Delivers a briefing to the attorney

Before the consultation happens, the attorney receives a structured intake summary — case type, key facts, statute-of-limitations notes, and the full call transcript if they want it. The attorney walks into the first call already knowing what they're dealing with. This is time that used to get eaten up by the attorney re-interviewing the client to fill gaps in the paralegal's notes.

How They Built It: A 4-Week Implementation

The firm's implementation followed a phased rollout that minimized disruption while allowing real-world testing before full deployment.

Week 1 — Discovery: The implementation team documented the firm's practice areas, existing intake scripts, attorney routing preferences, case management system (Clio), and calendar tools. They mapped out every intake scenario: car accident calls, slip-and-fall, wrongful death, workers' comp, and out-of-scope calls. Each scenario got its own intake script and routing logic.

Week 2 — Build and Integration: The agent was built, connected to Clio for conflict checking and calendar management, and tested internally with simulated calls covering edge cases — callers with incomplete information, calls in Spanish, calls with statute-of-limitations urgency, and calls that turned out to be out of scope.

Week 3 — Soft Launch: The agent went live for after-hours calls only (5 PM to 9 AM weekdays, all day weekends). This is the highest-value window — the exact time when leads were previously going straight to voicemail — and the lowest risk, since any errors wouldn't interrupt the business-hours workflow the team was used to.

Week 4 — Daytime Overflow: The agent was extended to handle overflow during business hours — picking up when the intake coordinator was on another call or stepped away. The coordinator remained the primary contact during business hours; the agent served as a parallel channel that never let a call go unanswered.

After 30 days, with the metrics proving out, the firm extended the agent to full coverage — all inbound calls, all hours — with the intake coordinator shifting her focus to follow-up, case preparation, and the calls the agent flagged as needing human handling.

The whole operation was stitched together using Make.com for workflow automation — connecting the intake agent output to Clio case creation, calendar booking, and the attorney briefing email. Building these connections in Make took a few hours rather than weeks of custom development.

The Results: What Changed

After 90 days of full deployment, here's what the firm measured:

  • 80% of inbound intake handled without human involvement. The intake coordinator — still on the team — shifted her time entirely to follow-up coordination, case prep support, and the 20% of calls the agent escalated. She described it as going from "constantly drowning" to "actually doing my job."
  • After-hours lead conversion went from 18% to 54%. This was the biggest single number. Those callers were previously reaching voicemail; now they were reaching a system that qualified and booked them on the spot. Several of the firm's highest-value signed cases in Q2 came from after-hours calls that would have gone nowhere under the old system.
  • 10–12 hours of attorney time recovered per week. Pre-agent, senior attorneys were regularly pulled into intake calls — either because the coordinator was busy, or because a caller called back and insisted on speaking with an attorney. The agent holds the line: it gathers what it needs, routes appropriately, and deflects calls that don't require attorney involvement.
  • Average time from call to booked consultation dropped from 4.2 hours to 6 minutes. The old workflow required a callback, then a scheduling conversation, then a calendar confirmation. The new workflow does it in one call.
  • Cost per qualified lead captured decreased by approximately 60%. The agent runs at a flat monthly cost — far below what a second intake hire would have cost — while handling 3–4× the call volume.

The firm's managing partner put it simply: "We didn't realize how much we were leaving on the table. The phone was ringing and nobody was home."

The Ethics and Compliance Layer

Law firms operate under ethical rules that most other businesses don't face, and AI intake deployments have to be built with that in mind. Three areas matter most.

Unauthorized Practice of Law

An AI intake agent must never give legal advice. It gathers information, answers questions about firm services and process, and routes calls — that's it. Any question about case merits, legal strategy, or rights gets deflected with "That's something the attorney will discuss with you at your consultation." The script has hard stops built in. Vendors who don't address this proactively aren't built for legal work.

Confidentiality

Intake calls contain sensitive client information. The agent's calls and transcripts must be encrypted, stored appropriately, and never used to train third-party AI models. This firm required a Business Associate Agreement (BAA)-equivalent data processing agreement from their vendor before deployment.

AI Disclosure

Many state bars now require that callers be informed they're speaking with AI — not a human. The agent in this deployment identifies itself at the start of every call: "Hi, this is the intake team at [Firm Name]. I'm an AI assistant — I'll gather some information for you and get the right attorney connected with you." Compliance varies by jurisdiction; check your state bar's current guidance before deploying.

Where the AI Stops (And Humans Take Over)

This is worth being honest about: AI intake has clear limits, and the firms deploying it well are the ones who've designed the human handoff thoughtfully.

The agent at this firm is configured to warm-transfer — immediately connect to a live person — in four scenarios:

  • Calls involving recent death or serious trauma. A caller who just lost a family member in an accident doesn't need a structured intake script — they need human warmth. The agent identifies these calls within the first 60 seconds and connects the caller to a paralegal or, if available, an attorney.
  • Statute-of-limitations emergencies. If a caller is within 48 hours of a filing deadline, the agent triggers an immediate alert to the on-call attorney. These don't wait for a callback.
  • Complex multi-party conflicts. If a conflict check surfaces something ambiguous — a former client's business partner, a related entity — the agent escalates to a paralegal for human review rather than making a call it's not equipped to make.
  • Callers who explicitly ask for a human. The agent respects this immediately. No badgering, no "Are you sure?" — just a clean transfer.

The result is a system where routine intake is fully automated, the coordinator's time is focused on the cases that need human judgment, and no caller who genuinely needs immediate human contact is left talking to a machine.

Your Move: Is This Right for Your Firm?

AI intake isn't just for large firms. The economics work especially well for firms with 2–10 attorneys, because that's where the gap between "calls that come in" and "calls someone can actually answer" is widest. A solo practitioner in depositions all day has the same after-hours problem as a 50-person firm — but less buffer staff to cover it.

Before you build or buy, here's what to audit first:

  1. Track your after-hours call volume for two weeks. How many calls are coming in outside business hours? What's your voicemail-to-signed-client conversion rate? This gives you a baseline to measure against.
  2. Map your intake script. What questions do you ask every caller? What determines whether a call gets qualified or disqualified? What routing rules do you actually use? This becomes the agent's operating logic — the better you can articulate it, the better the agent performs.
  3. Confirm your case management system has API access. Conflict checking and calendar booking require integration. Clio, MyCase, Filevine, and PracticePanther all support this. Older or niche systems may require a custom adapter.
  4. Check your state bar's AI disclosure requirements. This is a quick check, not a legal odyssey — most bar guidance is available online or from your state bar's ethics hotline.

The broader principle here isn't specific to law firms — it applies to any service business with a high-value, time-sensitive inbound lead flow. The cost of not answering fast is invisible until you measure it. Once you do, it's hard to unsee.
