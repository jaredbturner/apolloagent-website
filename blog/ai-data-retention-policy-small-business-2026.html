Your team can paste a customer contract into an AI assistant in seconds. The harder question is what happens to that contract afterward. Is it retained for abuse monitoring? Can an administrator delete it? Does a connected tool keep its own copy? Was the conversation created in a consumer account or a managed business workspace?

Those questions are no longer paperwork for large enterprises. They are operating decisions for any small business using AI with customer records, employee information, financial documents, or proprietary work. A practical AI data retention policy tells employees what they may submit, which approved tools they may use, how long data should remain available, and who verifies deletion.

Why “We Do Not Train on Your Data” Is Not Enough

Training and retention are different. A provider may promise not to train its models on business data while still retaining inputs and outputs for a defined period to operate the service, detect abuse, or support features such as conversation history. OpenAI's enterprise privacy page, for example, says API inputs and outputs are removed after 30 days by default unless legal retention is required, and that qualifying customers can request zero data retention for eligible endpoints.

Anthropic likewise documents retention controls for the Claude API and notes that zero data retention is available only for eligible products and configurations. The exact answer can also change when you add a server-side tool, code execution environment, file store, or third-party connector. That is why your review must cover the whole workflow, not just the model vendor's homepage.

The practical lesson is simple: never treat a privacy slogan as your policy. Record the product, account tier, endpoint or feature, default retention, available controls, deletion method, and contract terms. If you cannot answer those fields, sensitive data does not belong in that workflow yet.

Start with a Data-and-Tool Inventory

List every AI product people actually use, including browser assistants, meeting transcription, writing tools, CRM features, automation platforms, and custom API workflows. Shadow AI matters here: the free account an employee opened last month may carry more risk than the system your IT lead formally approved.

For each tool, capture the owner, business purpose, account type, data categories, connected systems, retention setting, deletion process, and renewal date. Then classify data into four useful buckets:

  • Public: published web copy, public product descriptions, and material intended for broad distribution.
  • Internal: ordinary operating documents that would be inconvenient, but not catastrophic, if exposed.
  • Confidential: customer records, contracts, pricing, source code, employee files, and nonpublic financial information.
  • Restricted: credentials, payment-card data, regulated health data, government identifiers, and information your contracts prohibit from entering a third-party AI system.

Public and internal data can usually move through approved business tools with normal controls. Confidential data needs a documented purpose and verified vendor settings. Restricted data should be blocked unless a specific, reviewed architecture and agreement permits it. Set Retention Rules by Business Need

Do not choose “keep forever” because it is the default. Decide how long each class of information needs to remain available. A support team may need a short conversation history to maintain continuity. A contract-analysis workflow may only need the document during processing, with the result stored in the company's document system. A sales assistant may need access to a CRM record without copying the full record into its own long-term memory.

Use the shortest period that still supports the job. That reduces the amount of data exposed during an account compromise, discovery request, or vendor incident. It also makes deletion believable. If the business needs a permanent record, store the approved output in the system of record and delete the working AI conversation on schedule.

Your policy should distinguish at least four clocks: provider retention, user-visible chat history, files or vector stores, and your own logs. Deleting a chat window may not delete an uploaded file, an API object, or data copied into an automation log. Conversely, a provider's zero-retention setting does not erase the copy you deliberately saved in your database.

Use a Seven-Question Vendor Checklist

  1. Is business data used for model training? Get the answer for your exact plan, not the consumer product.
  2. What is retained, and for how long? Ask separately about prompts, outputs, files, tool results, logs, and backups.
  3. Which features are excluded from zero retention? New tools may have different rules from basic text requests.
  4. Can administrators set retention centrally? Employee-by-employee settings are hard to audit.
  5. How is data deleted? Confirm whether deletion is immediate, queued, or subject to a backup window.
  6. Where is data processed? Location may matter to your customers, contracts, or regulator.
  7. Can you export audit evidence? You need to show which setting applied when a workflow ran.

Save the source URL, contract clause, and review date beside each answer. Vendor documentation changes. A quarterly review for high-risk tools and an annual review for low-risk tools is a reasonable starting cadence. Trigger an immediate review when a vendor adds memory, browsing, connectors, autonomous actions, or a new model tier.

This is also where current platform changes matter. OpenAI announced Private Safety Processing in 2026 as part of expanding zero-data-retention access for qualifying API customers. The relevant takeaway is not that every customer automatically has it; it is that privacy controls are becoming configuration-specific. Verify eligibility and endpoint coverage rather than assuming the newest control applies everywhere.

Build Retention into the Workflow

Policy without implementation becomes a PDF nobody follows. Put controls where the data moves. Use managed business accounts, single sign-on where available, role-based access, approved connectors, and separate development from production. Redact unnecessary personal information before sending a document to a model. Pass record IDs instead of full records when the agent can fetch only the fields it needs.

For automations built in Make.com or another workflow platform, inspect execution history as carefully as the model call. A scenario can copy a prompt, response, attachment, and error payload into its logs. Configure log retention where possible, restrict who can inspect runs, and avoid putting secrets or full restricted records into error messages.

Add deletion to the workflow definition. If a temporary file is created, name the step that removes it. If a conversation object is retained for follow-up, schedule its deletion after the business window closes. If deletion fails, send the exception to a named owner. Give Employees Rules They Can Remember

A usable employee policy can fit on one page. Name the approved tools. State that consumer accounts are not approved for confidential work. List the restricted data that may never be submitted. Require employees to check outputs before use and report accidental disclosure immediately. Provide a clear contact for exceptions.

Include examples drawn from real work: “You may summarize a public competitor page in the approved workspace.” “You may analyze a customer contract only in the approved contract workflow.” “You may not paste passwords, payment-card numbers, medical records, or a full employee file into a general assistant.” Concrete examples beat abstract warnings.

Train managers, not just individual contributors. Managers often create the pressure that leads to shortcuts: upload the spreadsheet, summarize the complaints, get the answer by noon. They need to know which approved path is fast enough to use under pressure.

Your First 30 Days

Week 1: inventory tools and classify the data entering them. Week 2: verify vendor training, retention, deletion, and feature-specific terms. Week 3: set approved tools and configure the shortest workable retention. Week 4: publish the employee rules, test deletion, and assign quarterly reviews.

Finally, test the policy with one real record. Follow it from source system to model, connector, logs, output, and deletion. If you cannot trace every copy, the workflow is not ready for confidential data.
