On September 9, HoneyBook announced it was live inside ChatGPT as a connected app — the latest addition to what OpenAI is now calling its Small Business Collection. That collection has quietly grown to 16 tools: Canva, Stripe, Gusto, Slack, Dropbox, ClickUp, Wix, and HoneyBook among them. The pitch is simple. Instead of opening eight different apps to answer one question about your business, you ask ChatGPT, and it pulls the answer from wherever the data actually lives.

This isn't a rebrand of the plugin store from a few years ago. OpenAI's own research found that at least four million people in the U.S. used ChatGPT during March 2026 to help plan, run, or grow a business — mostly not software founders, but consultants, retailers, contractors, and service businesses using it as a flexible stand-in for departments they can't afford to staff. The Small Business Collection is OpenAI's attempt to turn that organic usage into an actual product: connect your real tools, and ChatGPT stops being a generic assistant and starts being one that knows your invoices, your schedule, and your client list.

What Was Actually Announced

HoneyBook — a client relationship platform used by more than 100,000 service-based businesses to manage proposals, contracts, invoicing, and payments — is now a connected app inside ChatGPT. Members link their account, and ChatGPT can answer questions like which invoices are overdue, who's on this week's schedule, or which new leads should get answered first, then draft the replies, proposals, or payment requests that follow. Everything it produces lands as a draft. Nothing sends, changes, or issues without the business owner clicking approve.

That "connected app, drafts only" pattern is the same shape every other tool in the collection follows. Canva connects for design assets. Stripe connects for payments data. Gusto connects for payroll questions. Slack and Dropbox connect for internal communication and files. The common thread: your business software stays the system of record, and ChatGPT becomes the interface that reads across all of it and prepares the next action, rather than a 17th tool holding its own separate copy of your data.

Why This Is Different From a Chatbot Plugin

The plugin ecosystem OpenAI tried a few years back mostly failed because it asked users to discover, install, and remember which plugin did what — friction that killed adoption before habits formed. The Small Business Collection skips that problem by targeting owners who are already living inside ChatGPT for a dozen unrelated tasks a day, then giving them one-click access to the specific tools running their actual operations. You're not learning a new interface. You're pointing the interface you already use at data it previously couldn't see.

This is also a meaningful escalation from the model-only news we've been covering. Recent model upgrades have made ChatGPT better at reasoning through a task you hand it. The Small Business Collection makes ChatGPT aware of the actual state of your business without you having to explain it every time. Combine a more capable assistant with direct access to your invoicing, scheduling, and CRM data, and the assistant stops being something you prompt from scratch and starts behaving like a coordinator who already knows what's going on — which is the same trajectory we flagged when Salesforce and Anthropic wired Claude directly into the CRM.

The 16 Tools, By What They Actually Do

OpenAI hasn't published every name on the list, but the confirmed members split cleanly into four categories most small businesses will recognize from their own stack:

  • Client relationships and sales: HoneyBook (proposals, contracts, invoicing, payments for service businesses)
  • Money: Stripe (payments), Gusto (payroll)
  • Creative and web: Canva (design assets), Wix (website content and pages)
  • Operations and internal communication: Slack (messaging), Dropbox (files), ClickUp (project and task management)

Notice what's missing from that list: nothing here reaches directly into your bank account, your legal documents, or your customers' payment details without an explicit connection step you control. That's a deliberate design choice, and it's the right one — but it also means the value you get depends entirely on which of these 16 tools you already use, and how completely your business data lives inside them versus scattered across spreadsheets and email threads.

What To Connect First

If you're running a service business with client work, contracts, and invoicing, HoneyBook is the obvious starting point — it's the single connection most likely to eliminate the "let me check three different places" moment that eats up a founder's morning. If your business runs on projects and deadlines instead of client proposals, ClickUp will do more for you than any other tool on the list. If cash flow visibility is your actual pain point, Stripe alone — just being able to ask "which invoices are overdue" in plain English — is worth connecting before anything else.

Resist the urge to connect everything at once. The same lesson applies here that applied when we covered workspace AI agents moving into business apps: connecting a tool to an AI assistant isn't free. It's a new data-access relationship, and every one you add is one more thing to monitor, one more permission to review every few months, and one more system that needs a plan for what happens if the connection misbehaves.

Before You Connect Anything

The "draft only, approval required" design in HoneyBook's rollout is the right default, and it's worth checking whether every tool you connect actually holds to it. Ask directly: does this connection let ChatGPT send an email, issue a refund, or update a client record on its own, or does everything land as a draft for a human to review first? That single answer tells you whether you're adding a convenience or a liability.

Also check who else in your business can see or use the connection once it's live. A connected HoneyBook account tied to your login is different from one available to every employee with ChatGPT access — and if you haven't audited who has access to what inside your AI tools recently, our AI agent permission audit checklist walks through exactly what to check before you add one more connected app to the pile. The pattern we keep seeing across Meta's Hatch agent, Salesforce's Claude integration, and now this: every major platform is racing to connect an AI assistant to the tools that run your business, and the businesses that get burned aren't the ones that connect too slowly — they're the ones that connect without checking what the AI is actually allowed to do once it's in.

A 30-Minute Setup Checklist

If you want to try this today, here's the practical order of operations:

  1. Pick one tool, not all sixteen. Start with whichever holds the data you check most often — usually invoicing, scheduling, or a CRM.
  2. Connect it and ask a read-only question first. "Which invoices are overdue" or "who's on this week's schedule" — confirm ChatGPT is pulling accurate, current data before you ask it to draft anything.
  3. Test the draft-and-approve flow before trusting it. Have it draft a proposal or a reply, and check it against what you'd actually send — treat the first few drafts as a QA pass on the AI, not a shortcut.
  4. Set a calendar reminder to review the connection in 90 days. Confirm it's still only doing what you approved it to do, and that no one else quietly gained access.
  5. Only then add a second tool. One well-understood connection beats five you're not fully sure about.

The Small Business Collection is a genuinely useful shift — it's OpenAI meeting small business owners where they already spend their time instead of asking them to adopt yet another dashboard. But "useful" and "worth connecting everything immediately" are different claims. Pick the one tool that removes your biggest daily friction, confirm the draft-and-approve guardrails actually hold, and expand from there. That's the difference between an assistant that saves you an hour a day and one more integration you have to babysit.
